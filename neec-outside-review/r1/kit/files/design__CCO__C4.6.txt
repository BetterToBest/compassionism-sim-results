[Simulation v5.6: study.html] Two methods ask which inputs the answer depends on. Screening (the Morris method, 1991) ranks how far each input moves an outcome over its range, on 20 random paths of 20 paired runs (seeds) each. The follow-up Sobol run (the Sobol method, Saltelli et al. 2010) splits the spread of each outcome among the inputs, with 128 base points and 32 paired runs (seeds) per point, in Stress. Participation and the amount of BU paid explain most of the spread in poverty, BLEI poverty, cost and inflation. These are design choices about the program's size, not uncertain facts about the world. For basket poverty, the income effect on earnings is the one uncertain behavioral constant that matters. [Simulation v5.6: README.md] The page opens with Compassionism's results. It follows the same 500 simulated adults and their children, living in households, who can lose their jobs and find new ones. It runs them for 20 years (or 40, with the Years switch), once with the program and once with no program, in three environments (Reference, Adverse, Stress). Basic living covered leads: the BLEI measure, the days of basic living a person's resources cover. One plain sentence each follows on poverty, savings, children, who gains, work, prices, cost and how it is paid, and the public costs of homelessness avoided. Prices are given as the typical run with the range of eight runs in ten (the 10th to 90th percentile). Above 1,000 times today's prices, the page describes the figure as a limit of the model's price rule, not a forecast. A table sets the Research Hub's own Year 7 targets (poverty under 2%, Gini coefficient 0.25 to 0.30) beside what the model reaches. Every figure is an average over 500 paired runs, with its 95% interval, and the page names the command that reproduces it. A button runs the model live in the browser. The model measures poverty, cost and work, not the cultural effects Compassionism aspires to. Poverty is measured against a living-wage basket, a higher bar than extreme poverty.
