[Research Hub: cco-ptf-simulation-replication.html] Linear automation displacement model. Automation impact is modelled as a linear wage drag (population_rate × automationRisk) with no task-complexity structure, no new-job creation dynamics, and no occupational transition pathways. The model captures displacement magnitude only, not the labour market reallocation that historically accompanies technology transitions. automationRisk uniform distribution (added in v3.4). automationRisk ∈ [0.2, 1.0] is drawn from a uniform distribution. The uniform assumption may understate polarization in High Automation scenarios. Priority calibration refinement in CONTRIBUTING.md .

[Research Hub: cco-ptf-simulation-replication.html] Cumulative Poverty Exposure is aggregate, not longitudinal (new metric, v4.13). The two new exposure figures sum each year's population poverty share, which gives the mean years-poor per agent exactly — but says nothing about how those years are distributed across agents. "Every agent poor for three years" and "30% of agents poor for ten years" produce the same number and are very different policy situations. Distinguishing them needs per-agent poverty spells tracked over time, which the engine does not keep (agents carry current state, not history). The related measures the v4.12 audit also suggested — time-to-tier-attainment per agent, wealth-floor recovery rates, spell-length distributions — all depend on that same missing longitudinal layer and remain open; see CONTRIBUTING.md . [Simulation v5.6: study.html] The clearest miss is the safety net. In the data the safety net absorbed about 73% of the recession's rise in poverty (a rise of +5.77 points before taxes and transfers, +1.56 after). In the model it absorbs about 0.5% (a rise of +6.90 points without the safety net, +6.86 with it). The file's label is "same direction, different size", because the model's share is just above zero and the rule fixed in advance gives that label to any positive share; the gap is far outside the band of 20 points, so in substance it is a miss. Three reasons, all in the model's design: (a) SNAP and the tax credits are paid on last year's income, so they arrive a year late; (b) the model's cost-of-living line is high, and benefits of a few thousand dollars seldom lift someone over it (they shrink the gap instead); (c) the data's "before transfers" removes far more than the model's three programs (SNAP, the Earned Income Tax Credit and the refundable Child Tax Credit): it also removes unemployment insurance, Social Security, housing aid and the tax system, while in the model unemployment insurance is in both runs and the others do not respond to the shock. So in a downturn the model's baseline safety net is weaker than the real one, and a comparison of Compassionism against today's safety net in a recession should be read with that in mind. The model has no stimulus payments or weekly unemployment top-up, which is why the 2020 supplemental rate (reported, no verdict) fell in the data and rose in the model.
